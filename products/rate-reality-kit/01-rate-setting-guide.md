# Rate-Setting Guide

## Why your gut number is wrong

Most freelancers set their rate by copying a number they saw on a forum, or by taking their old salary and dividing by 2,000 hours. Neither accounts for the hours you don't bill (admin, proposals, scope creep) or the costs you didn't have as an employee (your own taxes, software, insurance, downtime). That's the exact gap the calculator just showed you.

Freelancers who switch from hourly to value-based pricing report a median income of roughly $96,000/year, against roughly $58,000/year for those still billing by the hour, on comparable work. The rate isn't the only lever — but it's the biggest one, and it's the one most people never touch because raising your rate is the single most commonly cited challenge among freelancers.

## Step 1 — Find your floor (hourly, for reference only)

Floor rate = (Target annual take-home + annual business expenses + annual tax owed) ÷ realistic annual billable hours.

Use the numbers from the free calculator — your "Rate You Need" output is this floor. Never quote this number to a client. It's the minimum below which you're losing money, not a price.

## Step 2 — Reprice around value, not hours

For each project, answer one question before you write a proposal: *what is this worth to the client if it works*, not *how long will it take me*. A landing page that converts has a value tied to revenue it generates, not to the four hours you spent in Figma. Quote a project price built from that value, with your hourly floor as a private sanity check, never as the pitch.

## Step 3 — Price in tiers, not a single number

Offer three packages (Essentials / Standard / Premium) for the same type of project. Most clients pick the middle one. This single change typically raises your average deal size more than any amount of "just charge more" willpower, because you're no longer negotiating a single number down — you're letting the client choose up.

## Step 4 — Re-price existing clients on a schedule, not a whim

Set a standing rule: rates increase for every client once every 6–12 months, announced 30 days ahead, framed as a scheduled adjustment ("as of [date], my rates are adjusting to X") rather than a negotiation. Clients who would leave over a scheduled, modest increase were rarely profitable anyway — see the client-screening checklist in this kit.

## Step 5 — Protect the number you just set

A rate increase on paper means nothing if scope creep quietly erodes it back down. Use the scope-creep scripts in this kit the first time a client says "while you're in there, can you also—".
